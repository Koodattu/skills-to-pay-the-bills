# Short-form narrative guidance

Use this file for voiceover videos under 60 seconds. The user-defined scope is under one minute even when a platform permits longer Shorts.

## Contents

- The one-idea rule

- Hook design
- Microstory engines

## The one-idea rule

A short-form script normally needs:

- one viewer promise;
- one dominant question, claim, transformation, or moment;
- one clear turn;
- one payoff.

Supporting facts may exist, but they must serve the same spine. Do not fit an entire long-form outline into 60 seconds.

The first spoken line should let the viewer identify the subject or situation and feel a concrete reason to continue. Mystery without orientation is usually confusion.

## Flexible timing structures

These are planning defaults, not fixed templates.

### 15 seconds

- 0-1s: hook and subject
- 1-5s: essential setup
- 5-11s: development or demonstration
- 11-14s: turn and payoff
- 14-15s: button, if needed

### 30 seconds

- 0-2s: hook and orientation
- 2-7s: stakes or baseline expectation
- 7-20s: evidence, attempt, mechanism, or escalation
- 20-27s: turn and payoff
- 27-30s: consequence or button

### 45-60 seconds

- 0-2s: hook and subject
- 2-8s: minimum viable context and stakes
- 8-30s: development, proof, or escalating attempts
- 30-45s: complication, reversal, or decisive evidence
- 45-56s: payoff and consequence
- final seconds: button or tightly matched call to action

Move the payoff earlier when the central value is a demonstration, answer, or result. Do not delay a simple answer for 55 seconds if the delay adds no story.

## Hook design

Useful short-form hooks include:

- **Outcome first**: “This bridge moved nearly a meter before anyone understood why.”
- **Concrete anomaly**: show or name the fact that should not be possible.
- **Immediate conflict**: goal, obstacle, and consequence in one or two lines.
- **Expectation reversal**: establish a familiar belief and break it with evidence.
- **Specific high-value question**: ask something the video can answer quickly.
- **Before and after**: define a striking transformation and imply the route.
- **Proof in hand**: begin with the object, result, recording, number, or test.
- **Compressed confession or discovery**: reveal the narrator's costly mistake or surprising finding without throat-clearing.

A hook should be understandable without the caption, though captions may strengthen it. Do not rely on “stop scrolling,” “wait for it,” “you need to see this,” or generic disbelief language.

## Microstory engines

### In-medias-res story

Moment in motion → minimum context → complication → decision or turn → consequence.

### Answer-first explainer

Answer or surprising claim → mechanism → proof or example → implication.

### Myth correction

Specific belief → why it seems plausible → decisive correction → useful replacement model.

### Failure-to-fix

Goal → mistake or failure → discovered constraint → changed approach → result.

### Reveal

Concrete clue → likely interpretation → conflicting clue → actual explanation → recontextualized opening.

### Ranked or listed content with a spine

State the selection rule → move quickly through items → escalate toward the most consequential item → explain why it wins. Do not present interchangeable facts with arbitrary numbering.

### One-moment profile

Defining action or quote → essential identity → pressure or choice → consequence. Do not summarize an entire life.

## Compression

Compress by changing the architecture, not by speaking unnaturally fast.

Use these operations in order:

1. Delete greetings, topic announcements, and repeated promises.
2. Select one idea; remove secondary theses and subplots.
3. Enter later: start at the consequential moment.
4. Collapse context into the one detail that makes the turn intelligible.
5. Replace abstract explanation with one concrete example or comparison.
6. Combine sentences that repeat the same function.
7. Remove transition phrases when causality is already clear.
8. Cut the weaker of two examples.
9. Move the proof next to the claim.
10. End immediately after the payoff or consequence.

Do not delete the causal link that explains why the outcome happened. A fast sequence of disconnected facts feels dense but is hard to retain.

## Payoff and ending

Write the payoff before finalizing the hook. The opening should create the exact expectation the ending can fulfill.

A short-form payoff may be:

- the answer;
- the result of a test;
- the hidden mechanism;
- the decisive mistake;
- the transformation;
- the final comparison;
- the reversal of the opening assumption;
- the practical action the viewer can now take.

The final line should add consequence, meaning, or closure. Do not repeat the thesis in grander language.

A looped ending is optional. Use it only when the final phrase naturally completes, reframes, or grammatically reconnects to the opening. Never sacrifice clarity or payoff for replay bait.

Calls to action should be brief, specific, and placed after the value. Prefer a next step related to the subject over “like and follow for more.” Omit the call to action when it weakens the last beat.

## Timing and delivery

A 60-second script often begins in the range of roughly 130-165 spoken words, but pace varies materially by narrator, language, pauses, humor, and complexity. A 30-second script is not automatically half the words if it needs time for a reveal or visual demonstration.

Perform the actual read. Short-form delivery often sounds faster than long-form, but comprehension still governs.

During revision, test:

- Can the first line be understood on first hearing?
- Does every sentence change the viewer state?
- Is the central subject named early enough?
- Does the turn arrive before the viewer can predict the whole script?
- Is the payoff clear before any call to action?
- Can difficult names or numbers be spoken at the intended pace?
- Is any sentence present only because it sounds dramatic?
