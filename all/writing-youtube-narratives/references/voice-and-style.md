# Voice and style guidance

Use this file when defining a channel voice, adapting supplied samples, or fixing narration that sounds generic, overwritten, or difficult to perform.

## Build a channel voice profile

Infer or define:

- **Narrator role:** witness, investigator, teacher, participant, critic, comedian, documentarian, or hybrid
- **Authority:** tentative, peer-level, expert, omniscient only when evidence permits
- **Distance:** intimate, conversational, observational, formal, deadpan
- **Energy:** restrained, urgent, playful, eerie, reflective, confrontational
- **Information density:** low, medium, high
- **Sentence rhythm:** clipped, mixed, flowing, deliberately variable
- **Humor:** mechanism, frequency, targets, and forbidden contexts
- **Explanation depth:** assumed knowledge and acceptable jargon
- **Emotional range:** which emotions the narrator permits and how directly they are named
- **Signature constraints:** phrases, structures, and tonal habits to avoid

Use `templates/channel-profile.md` when the profile should persist across scripts.

## Learn from samples

When given sample scripts or transcripts:

1. Ignore topic-specific wording.
2. Measure the recurring structural traits.
3. Note median sentence length, frequency of fragments, questions, analogies, asides, and punchlines.
4. Identify how the narrator establishes credibility.
5. Identify how sections begin and end.
6. Identify the humor mechanism: reversal, exaggeration, understatement, absurd specificity, callback, edit-dependent juxtaposition, or self-implication.
7. Record phrases and signature bits that must not be copied.
8. Produce a compact profile, then write new material from the profile rather than from surface mimicry.

## Natural spoken prose

Spoken narration is linear and ephemeral. Make it easy to process once.

- Put the subject and verb near the beginning when a sentence carries new information.
- Avoid long noun stacks and deeply nested clauses.
- Place the important word near the end of a sentence when it benefits emphasis.
- Use parallel structure for comparisons and lists.
- Repeat a key noun when a pronoun would be ambiguous.
- Break before a technical term, number, name, or reversal that needs emphasis.
- Prefer a concrete example before an abstract generalization when the audience is unfamiliar with the topic.
- Let a short sentence follow a dense one.
- Use silence, not filler, when a moment needs weight.

Do not make every line five words long. Artificially clipped prose creates a monotonous “trailer voice” and wastes the contrast that gives short lines force.

## Specificity

Prefer details that alter the viewer's mental picture or causal model.

Weak: “The project faced many serious problems.”

Stronger: “By launch week, the battery overheated after eleven minutes, the supplier had missed two shipments, and the team had one prototype left.”

Do not pile on sensory detail merely to sound literary. Use details that establish scale, pressure, character, evidence, or consequence.

## Emotion

Create emotion through situation, choice, contrast, and consequence before naming it.

Prefer:

- what a person did under pressure;
- what changed in the room, plan, body, or outcome;
- the cost of a decision;
- an object or detail that carries meaning;
- a gap between expectation and reality.

Use explicit emotional labels when they clarify rather than substitute for evidence.

## Humor

Humor should release, sharpen, characterize, or reframe. It should not interrupt the causal chain.

Useful mechanisms:

- precise understatement after an extreme fact;
- an analogy that clarifies and surprises;
- a callback that rewards attention;
- self-implication rather than detached mockery;
- escalation from reasonable to absurd;
- a hard contrast between formal framing and mundane reality;
- one unusually specific word where a generic one was expected.

Avoid a joke after every fact. Do not joke through injury, grief, abuse, or other sensitive material unless the user has established an appropriate voice and the humor does not target those harmed.

## Authority and uncertainty

Sound certain only where the evidence is certain.

- “The data shows” requires direct, relevant data.
- “The evidence suggests” permits a supported inference.
- “One possibility is” marks a hypothesis.
- “We do not know” is preferable to false closure.

A qualified claim can still be narratively strong when the uncertainty itself is the tension.

## Transitional language

Write the logical relationship, not a generic transition.

Instead of “Now let's talk about the second problem,” use the relationship:

- “Solving the heat problem created a weight problem.”
- “That explanation worked until the second test.”
- “The result looked like a success, but it removed the only safety margin.”
- “To understand why, we have to go back three years.”

Use explicit signposts when the topic is complex, but do not announce every section like a presentation agenda.

## Common generic patterns to remove

Flag and usually rewrite:

- “Hey guys” / “Welcome back”
- “In today's video”
- “Have you ever wondered”
- “Picture this” when no precise scene follows
- “But here's the thing”
- “What happened next changed everything”
- “Little did they know”
- “Let that sink in”
- “Buckle up”
- “This isn't just X. It's Y.”
- “It was a testament to...”
- “Against all odds” without establishing the odds
- “A story of hope, courage, and determination”
- “The answer may surprise you”
- “And the rest is history”

These phrases are not forbidden in every context. They are forbidden as automatic substitutes for a concrete relationship, fact, or voice-specific line.
