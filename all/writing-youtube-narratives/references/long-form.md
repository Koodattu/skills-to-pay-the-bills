# Long-form narrative guidance

Use this file for videos longer than 60 seconds, especially narrative videos around 8-15 minutes.

## The long-form contract

A long-form opening must do more than attract attention. Within roughly the first 30 seconds, it should let a new viewer understand:

1. What this video is actually about
2. What is unusual, useful, difficult, or consequential about it
3. What question, goal, conflict, or transformation will organize the video
4. What kind of payoff the viewer can reasonably expect

Do not answer every question immediately. Give enough of an answer to establish trust and enough uncertainty to create forward pressure.

## Flexible 10-minute architecture

Treat these timings as a planning scaffold, not a formula.

### 0:00-0:08 — Cold open

Enter at the point of maximum useful tension: a result, contradiction, consequential moment, striking piece of evidence, or precise unanswered question.

### 0:08-0:30 — Promise and stakes

Orient the viewer. Connect the cold open to the title and thumbnail. Define why the outcome, explanation, or journey matters.

### 0:30-1:30 — Minimum viable context

Give only the context needed to understand the first meaningful development. Establish the initial story state, key actors, constraints, and baseline expectation.

### 1:30-3:15 — First development and partial payoff

Provide proof that the premise has substance. Answer an early question, demonstrate the mechanism, or complete the first attempt. The answer should expose a consequence or deeper problem.

### 3:15-5:30 — Complication or expansion

Challenge the easy explanation. Introduce a reversal, hidden constraint, competing account, failed attempt, larger scale, or higher cost.

### 5:30-7:45 — Escalation and convergence

Bring separate threads into causal contact. Narrow the plausible explanations or choices. Make the endpoint feel increasingly inevitable, difficult, or meaningful.

### 7:45-9:15 — Climax, decisive proof, or main reveal

Resolve the central question through the strongest event, evidence, demonstration, decision, or synthesis. Do not postpone the real answer until the last sentence merely to create a cliffhanger.

### 9:15-10:00 — Consequence, meaning, and button

Show what changed and why it matters. Resolve remaining essential setups. End on one strong implication, image-shaped thought, callback, or next action. Stop before the energy drains into a recap.

## Beat design

Plan 7-12 substantial beats for a 10-minute script. A beat can be a scene, claim, attempt, discovery, reversal, mechanism, counterargument, or consequence.

For each beat, know:

- **Purpose**: why it exists
- **Viewer state before**: what the viewer knows and expects
- **New value**: the event, evidence, explanation, or emotion added
- **Turn**: what changes by the end
- **Payoff**: what earlier setup it answers, if any
- **Bridge**: why the next beat follows

A useful beat map format:

| Time | Beat | Live viewer question | New information or event | Emotional or conceptual turn | Payoff / bridge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Do not make every beat equal in length. Important proof and emotional moments need room; routine context does not.

## Openings

Generate several openings from different mechanisms:

- **Result first**: show the consequence, then ask how it happened.
- **Scene in motion**: begin at a decision, failure, discovery, or irreversible moment.
- **Contradiction**: place the common expectation beside the fact that breaks it.
- **Specific question**: ask something answerable whose answer has clear stakes.
- **Proof first**: lead with a measurement, object, quote, test, or observation that makes the premise credible.
- **Compressed journey**: state the starting condition, obstacle, and remarkable endpoint without explaining the route.
- **Thesis under pressure**: make a strong claim, then immediately reveal the complication it must survive.

Avoid hooks that could introduce almost any video. “This changed everything,” “the truth will shock you,” and “you won't believe what happened” create no useful mental model.

## Middle progression

The middle should not be “more information.” It should be a sequence of changing story states.

Use this recurring motion:

1. Establish a local goal, question, or explanation.
2. Develop it with a scene, mechanism, example, or evidence.
3. Deliver a partial payoff.
4. Reveal its consequence, limitation, exception, or new obstacle.
5. Bridge causally into the next beat.

The viewer should regularly receive answers, not merely promises of answers. A good partial payoff increases trust and makes the next question more valuable.

Use background only when it changes how the next development is understood. Move late-occurring but highly compelling material earlier when doing so does not distort causality or spoil the intended experience.

## Re-engagement

Re-engagement is a change in the viewer's model, not a stock phrase.

Useful re-engagement moves include:

- resolving a question and exposing a more consequential one;
- revealing evidence that invalidates the current explanation;
- changing scale from one person to a system, or back;
- returning to the cold open with new context;
- introducing a cost, deadline, constraint, or irreversible choice;
- switching from claim to demonstration;
- presenting the strongest reasonable counterargument;
- showing that the apparent success created the later failure;
- narrowing several possibilities to two, then one.

Do not add “But here's the crazy part” unless the next line genuinely changes the story and the wording fits the channel.

## Payoffs and endings

Use payoffs at three scales:

- **Beat payoff**: answers the local question.
- **Section payoff**: resolves a phase of the narrative.
- **Video payoff**: fulfills the central promise.

The main payoff should be specific enough that the viewer could describe what they learned, saw, or felt. “It was all about perseverance” is not a payoff unless the story has demonstrated a precise, non-obvious form of perseverance.

Strong ending options:

- **Consequence**: what the result changed afterward
- **Recontextualization**: the opening now means something different
- **Synthesis**: several facts become one clear model
- **Choice**: the viewer understands the decision that remains
- **Callback**: an early detail returns with earned meaning
- **Image-shaped thought**: a concrete final observation carries the theme
- **Clean practical takeaway**: one action or principle, without a list recap

Place a call to action after the narrative payoff, not in front of it. Make it specific to the value just delivered. Omit it when it weakens the ending or the user did not request one.

## Timing

Estimate only as a starting point:

`spoken words = target minutes × calibrated words per minute`

For an uncalibrated 10-minute explanatory or documentary voiceover, begin around 1,350-1,550 spoken words. Faster comedy, gaming, or dense technical narration may differ; reflective or dramatic work may require substantially fewer words.

Then perform a timed read. Account for:

- pauses and emphasis;
- difficult names and numbers;
- quoted audio or silence;
- laughter or reactions;
- room for the visual evidence to register;
- intentional changes of pace.

Do not pad a short script to hit time. Add a missing causal step, example, counterargument, consequence, or scene only when it improves the story.

## Structure-specific notes

### Documentary or history

Build around pressure, decisions, causes, and consequences. Do not pretend the audience is watching events unfold live when the outcome is common knowledge unless dramatic irony is intentional. Avoid invented scene detail.

### Explainer or video essay

Give the surprising claim or practical problem early. Move from observation to mechanism to evidence to implication. Include the strongest useful complication rather than presenting a frictionless thesis.

### Challenge or experiment

Clarify the goal, rules, constraints, and failure condition. Every attempt should alter the strategy or stakes. Avoid a montage-like list of attempts with no learning.

### Profile or biography

Organize around a defining contradiction, pursuit, choice, or cost. A life is not a chronology. Select events that causally explain the transformation or legacy.

### Investigation or mystery

State what is known, what is uncertain, and why the uncertainty matters. Reveal evidence in an order that preserves fair inference. Do not hide facts the narrator already knows solely to manufacture surprise.
